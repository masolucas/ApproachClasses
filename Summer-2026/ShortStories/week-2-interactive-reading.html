The Kiss.

Anton Chekhov  ·  Adapted Version  ·  Unit 1: The Human Heart

At eight o’clock on the evening of May twentieth, six artillery batteries stopped for the night in the small village of Myestetchki. While the officers were busy with their horses and guns, a stranger on a horse appeared near the church. He took off his hat and said:

“General von Rabbek invites you gentlemen to come and drink tea with him.”

Then he turned his horse and disappeared.

“What does this mean?” the officers grumbled. “We’re tired, and now we have to go drink tea with some general!”

They remembered a similar invitation the year before. An old count had invited them to his house, fed them well, and then kept them awake all night telling long stories about his youth. Would this von Rabbek be the same? There was no way to refuse, so the officers changed their uniforms and walked together toward the general’s house.

They found the house at the end of a long road lined with stone storehouses. Von Rabbek himself, a distinguished-looking man of about sixty, met them at the door. He said he was very glad to see them, but he apologized — his house was already full of visiting family, so he could not offer the officers a place to sleep. His wife, a tall woman with a graceful, practiced smile, said much the same thing. It was clear that both of them were only inviting the officers because good manners required it, not because they truly wanted the company.

Inside, about a dozen guests sat at a long table having tea. The officers, feeling awkward, sat down among them. The shyest of them all was Ryabovitch — a small officer in glasses, with sloping shoulders and thin side-whiskers. He had always felt himself to be the least interesting man in the room, and tonight was no exception. At first he could hardly focus on anything — the faces, the lamps, the conversation all blurred together. Slowly, though, his eyes adjusted, and he began to notice the confident, easy manner of von Rabbek’s family as they drew the officers into a lively argument about which branch of the army had the easier life.

After tea, everyone moved to another room, where a piano began to play a waltz. Ryabovitch had never once danced in his life, and had never once put his arm around a woman’s waist. He watched the other officers dance and felt no jealousy anymore — only a quiet sadness, the kind that had become familiar to him over the years.

When one of von Rabbek’s sons invited two officers to play billiards, Ryabovitch followed, simply because he had nothing else to do. But in the billiard room, no one paid him any attention, and after a while he felt he was only in the way. He decided to go back to the drawing room.

On the way, he took a wrong turn. He walked through several unfamiliar rooms, and finally opened a door into complete darkness. Through a crack in a far doorway, he could see a thin line of light, and the sound of music came faintly from the other side. He stood still, unsure which way to go.

Suddenly he heard fast footsteps, and a woman’s breathless voice whispered, “At last!” Two soft arms wrapped around his neck. A warm cheek pressed against his. He heard the small sound of a kiss.

Then the woman gasped — clearly realizing her mistake — and rushed away. Ryabovitch, just as startled, hurried toward the light at the door.

When he returned to the drawing room, his hands were shaking so much that he hid them behind his back. At first he was afraid that everyone could somehow tell what had just happened to him. But no one even looked at him twice, and slowly his fear turned into something else — a strange, powerful joy that he had never felt before. His neck still seemed to tingle where her arms had been. He wanted to laugh, to dance, to run outside. For the first time, he forgot that he was shy and unremarkable. He even smiled so warmly at von Rabbek’s wife that she looked at him in surprise.

At supper, Ryabovitch ate without noticing what was on his plate. He kept turning the mystery over in his mind. Some woman, he decided, must have been waiting in the dark for a lover, grew impatient, and mistook him for the man she was expecting. But who was she? He studied the faces of the young women at the table, trying to guess. He looked at one woman’s shoulders, another’s voice, a third’s laugh, and tried to build the face of his mystery woman out of pieces — but no single face matched all of it. The image stayed blurred, always slipping away.

Later, walking home through the dark garden with the other officers, no one spoke for a while. Ryabovitch guessed that they were all thinking the same private thought: would they, too, one day have a house like this, a family, a life grand enough to invite strangers in for tea?

That night, back in his quarters, Ryabovitch lay in bed replaying the moment in his mind — the footsteps, the whisper, the kiss — and felt an unreasonable happiness rise in him again. His roommate Lobytko came in loudly, annoyed that his servant hadn’t been able to find any beer, and eventually went out to search for some himself. The other roommate, Merzlyakov, a quiet and serious man, read his newspaper by candlelight and said nothing.

The next morning, the joy was still there. A few days later, unable to keep it to himself any longer, Ryabovitch told his roommates the whole story. Lobytko, who rarely believed anything, just laughed. Merzlyakov, without even looking up from his paper, said flatly, “She must be some sort of hysterical woman, throwing herself at a stranger like that.” Ryabovitch quietly agreed, then walked away and promised himself he would never share the story again.

Camp life continued, day after day. But something in Ryabovitch had changed. Every morning he woke with the sense that something warm and good was in his life. In the evenings, when the other officers talked about women, he listened closely, as a soldier listens to stories of a battle he was once part of. And whenever he joined the others on their evening outings into town, he felt strangely guilty afterward, as though he had betrayed someone. In quiet moments, he thought constantly of Myestetchki — the strange horse, von Rabbek’s tired smile, the dark room, the line of light under the door.

Weeks later, his unit marched back the same way, and Ryabovitch felt his heart racing with hope. Surely, he thought, he would see her again — or at least walk through that same dark room and remember. As the familiar church came into view, his heart pounded.

But this time, no messenger came riding out to invite them to tea.

Ryabovitch waited in his quarters, sure that word would come at any moment. It did not. Finally, unable to sit still, he walked alone to the church, then down the same path toward the river, then to the garden gate. He looked in. It was completely dark and silent. He waited a long while, hoping for a light, a voice, anything — but nothing came.

He walked down to the little bridge by the riverbank and stood there, looking at the water moving quickly past in the moonlight. Now that he expected nothing, the whole adventure suddenly looked small and plain to him. Of course he would never see her again. It would have been strange if he had.

The water flowed on beneath him, going nowhere in particular, just as it had done in May. And looking up at the sky, Ryabovitch felt, for a moment, that his whole life — his hopes, his daydreams, the kiss itself — was a kind of pointless, unremarkable joke. He thought of his summer of daydreaming and felt his life was plain and a little sad.

Back at his quarters, he learned that his roommates had gone to von Rabbek’s — a messenger had come, after all, but only after Ryabovitch had already given up and gone home. For one instant, hope flickered in his chest.

Then he pushed it down, got into bed, and — almost to spite his own bad luck — did not go.
