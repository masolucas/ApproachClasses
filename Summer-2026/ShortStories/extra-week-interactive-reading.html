The Book That Started with an Argument.

Sir Hugh Beaver  ·  Adapted Nonfiction Narrative  ·  Bonus Session

In November 1951, a man named Sir Hugh Beaver went shooting with friends in the countryside of Ireland. It was a cold, quiet evening, and the group was walking near a river when a bird flew past them, very fast.

“What kind of bird was that?” someone asked.

“A golden plover,” Beaver said. “And it’s the fastest game bird in Europe.”

Another man in the group disagreed. “No,” he said. “The grouse is faster.”

The two men argued about it for the rest of the evening. Neither one could prove he was right. There was no book, no encyclopedia, nothing anywhere that could answer a simple question like: which bird is faster?

This bothered Beaver. He was the managing director of the Guinness Brewery, a large and successful company, and he was used to solving problems. That night, he had an idea. He thought: people everywhere must have arguments like this one — in pubs, at dinner tables, after football matches. Someone should write a book that settles these arguments with facts.

For a few years, the idea stayed just an idea. But Beaver didn’t forget it. In 1954, he decided to act. He knew two young men in London, twin brothers named Norris and Ross McWhirter, who ran a small company that answered strange and difficult questions for newspapers and radio programs. If anyone could find facts about the fastest bird, the tallest man, or the longest river, it was the McWhirter twins.

Beaver hired them to create a book of world records. The brothers worked quickly. They wrote to scientists, athletes, explorers, and experts all over the world, asking for facts and numbers. They checked every detail carefully, because they knew that once something was printed in a book, people would believe it.

The first edition of The Guinness Book of Records was published in August 1955. It was small, only 198 pages, and at first it wasn’t for sale — Guinness gave it away free to pubs across Britain and Ireland. The idea was simple: give pub owners a way to end arguments, and remind customers of the Guinness name at the same time. It was, in a way, just clever advertising.

But something unexpected happened. The book became far more popular than anyone imagined. People loved reading about the world’s tallest man, the coldest temperature ever measured, the largest cake ever baked. Some facts were serious science; others were strange and funny. Readers couldn’t stop turning the pages. By Christmas of that year, the book was the best-selling non-fiction title in Britain.

Guinness quickly stopped giving the book away for free. Instead, they began selling it — and it kept selling, year after year. New editions came out every year, with new records added and old ones broken. People began sending letters to the McWhirter brothers, claiming their own records: the most pancakes eaten in one minute, the longest fingernails, the highest number of push-ups in an hour. Ordinary people started trying to get into the book, not just read it.

By the 1970s, The Guinness Book of Records was famous around the world, translated into dozens of languages. It later became known simply as Guinness World Records. Today, millions of copies are sold every year, and the book holds a record of its own — it is one of the best-selling books in history, alongside the Bible and the works of Shakespeare.

What began as a disagreement about a bird, on one cold evening in Ireland, grew into something Hugh Beaver could never have predicted: a book that would outlive him, entertain generations of readers, and give ordinary people around the world a strange but wonderful reason to attempt the impossible.
